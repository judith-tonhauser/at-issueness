\begin{abstract}
  At-issueness is a key concept in semantics and pragmatics, distinguishing the main point of an utterance from background information. However, there is no consensus on how at-issueness should be defined or diagnosed, given that different diagnostics for at-issueness align with different definitions and rely on different assumptions about properties of at-issue content. This paper presents an experimental comparison of five diagnostics of at-issueness, applied to the contents of non-restrictive relative clauses and the complements of clause-embedding predicates. The results are mixed: While some comparisons of the results of diagnostics reveal differences, suggesting that the diagnostics are not interchangeable, other comparisons yield more consistent results. The paper develops several hypotheses about factors that may lead different diagnostics to give different results and cautions against concluding that differences between the results of two diagnostics means that they probe distinct underlying properties (cf., \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017,koev_notions_2018}).
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords} definitions of at-issueness, properties of at-issue content, at-issueness diagnostics, experimental pragmatics, non-restrictive relative clauses, clause-embedding predicates
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:1_introduction}
\textit{At-issueness} is a key concept in theoretical semantics and pragmatics,\footnote{\citet{potts_presupposition_2015} credits the term to Bill Ladusaw, \enquote{who began using it informally in his UCSC undergraduate classes in 1985} (p.~2).} used in the analysis of various phenomena including presupposition, conventional implicature, evidentials, expressives, NPI licensing, and co-speech gestures (e.g., \citealt{faller_semantics_2002,faller_discourse_2019,horn_assertoric_2002,potts_logic_2005,simons_what_2010,lee_evidentiality_2011,tonhauser_how_2018,esipova_composition_2019,korotkova_evidential_2020,barnes_information_2023}). The concept is generally understood as distinguishing the main point of an utterance (the at-issue content) from propositions conveying background information (not-at-issue content). However, there is no consensus on how at-issueness is defined, and different diagnostics reflect different assumptions about properties of at-issue content (e.g., \citealt{tonhauser_diagnosing_2012,snider_at-issueness_2017,snider_anaphoric_2017,snider_distinguishing_2018,tonhauser_how_2018,koev_notions_2018,faller_discourse_2019,esipova_composition_2019,korotkova_evidential_2020}). %Consequently, it is an open question whether different diagnostics yield consistent results and whether they target the same underlying notion. %Consequently, empirical claims about the at-issueness of contents may be relative to specific diagnostics, and different diagnostics may not target the same underlying phenomenon.

%\subsection{Definitions of at-issueness}
Here, we discuss two prominent definitions: the QUD-based definition (\citealp{amaral_review_2007,simons_what_2010}) and the proposal-based definition (\citealt{murray_evidentiality_2010,murray_varieties_2014,anderbois_crossing_2010,anderbois_at-issue_2015,koev_apposition_2013}).\footnote{Further definitions proposed in the literature include those based on restrictive vs.\ non-restrictive modification (\citealt{esipova_composition_2019,esipova_not-at-issueness_2021}), and coherence-based discourse structure (\citealt{hunter_shapes_2016,jasinskaja_not_2016,koev_notions_2018}). %Clarifying the relationships among the various proposed definitions of at-issueness remains an important question for future research.
} The first one, given in \ref{def:qai}, assumes that discourse is structured around \textit{Questions Under Discussion} (QUDs; \citealt{roberts_information_2012,ginzburg_interrogatives_1996}) and defines at-issue content as utterance content intended to address a QUD (\citealp{amaral_review_2007,simons_what_2010}):

\ex. \label{def:qai}%
QUD at-issueness: \hfill (based on \citealt{simons_what_2010}, p. 323)\\
A content $m$ is at-issue in a context $c$ iff
\a. \label{def:qai-relevant}%
$m$ is relevant\footnotemark\ to the QUD in $c$, and
\b.  \label{def:qai-conventional}%
% $m$ is appropriately conventionally marked relative to the QUD.
the speaker has a recognizable intention to address the QUD via $m$.
\z. 
\z.

\footnotetext{$m$ may be a proposition or a question denotation, and relevance to the QUD can be defined in terms of contextual entailment of a partial or complete answer (\citealt{roberts_information_2012,simons_what_2010}), or based on inferences about the probability of QUD-answers (\citealt{buring_d-trees_2003,beaver_sense_2008}).
% \ex. Relevance to the QUD in context $c$ \hfill (based on \citealt{simons_what_2010}, p. 316)
% \a. A proposition $p$ is relevant the QUD, iff it contextually entails in $c$ a partial or complete answer to the QUD.
% \b. A question $q$ is relevant to the QUD, iff it has an answer that is relevant to the QUD.
% \z. \z.
}

The second one, given in \Next, is grounded in understanding assertions as proposals to update the common ground (\citealt{stalnaker_assertion_1978,clark_contributing_1989,ginzburg_resolving_1995,farkas_reacting_2010}). Here, the at-issue content of an assertion is defined as the content that is proposed for being added (\citealt{murray_evidentiality_2010,murray_varieties_2014,anderbois_crossing_2010,anderbois_at-issue_2015,koev_apposition_2013}), while not-at-issue content is already entailed by the common ground (e.g., \citealt{stalnaker_presuppositions_1973,stalnaker_common_2002}) or added to the common ground without being proposed (as in \citealt{murray_evidentiality_2010,murray_varieties_2014,anderbois_crossing_2010,anderbois_at-issue_2015}).

\ex. \label{def:proposal}%
Proposal at-issueness: \hfill (\citealt{koev_apposition_2013}, pp. 50--51)\\
A proposition $p$ is at-issue in a context $c$ iff
\a. $p$ is a proposal in $c$ and
\b. $p$ has not been accepted or rejected in $c$.
\z.
\z.

%\subsection{At-issueness diagnostics}

Diagnostics of at-issueness operationalize the definitions in \LLast and \Last via properties of at-issue content. Three properties, summarized in \citealt{tonhauser_diagnosing_2012}, are given in \ref{properties}: 

\ex.\label{properties} Properties of at-issue content (\citealt{tonhauser_diagnosing_2012})
\a.\label{properties.i} At-issue content addresses the question under discussion (e.g.,  \citealt{amaral_review_2007,simons_what_2010}).
\b.\label{properties.ii} The at-issue content of questions determines the relevant set of alternatives.
\c.\label{properties.iii} At-issue content can be directly assented or dissented with (e.g., \citealt{shanon_two_1976,groenendijk_studies_1984,faller_semantics_2002,faller_evidentiality_2006,murray_evidentiality_2010}).
\z.

While \citealt{tonhauser_diagnosing_2012} assumed that diagnostics based on all three properties in \ref{properties} are sensitive to QUD at-issueness, as defined in \ref{def:qai}, other works assume that diagnostics based on properties \ref{properties.i} and \ref{properties.ii} reflect QUD at-issueness, whereas diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.iii} are either assumed to reflect proposal at-issueness in \ref{def:proposal} (see, e.g., \citealt{koev_notions_2018}) or are sensitive to anaphoric availability rather than at-issueness (see, e.g., \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017,korotkova_evidential_2020}). 

Four commonly used diagnostics that build on the properties in \Last are illustrated in (\pref{qud})--(\pref{yesbut}) for sentence-medial non-restrictive relative clauses (NRRCs), which are typically taken to contribute not-at-issue content (\citealt{potts_logic_2005}). The QUD diagnostic in \ref{qud} is based on property \ref{properties.i}, the `asking whether' diagnostic in \ref{aw} is based on property \ref{properties.ii}, and the direct-dissent and `yes, but' diagnostics in \ref{dd} and \ref{yesbut} are based on property \ref{properties.iii}.

\ex. \label{qud}%
QUD diagnostic%
\a.[{\bf Nora: }] \emph{What did Greg buy?}
\b.[{\bf Leo: }] \emph{Greg, who bought a new car, is envied by his neighbor.}
\z.
Question to participants: How well does Leo's response fit Nora's question?
\z.

\ex. \label{aw}%
`asking whether' diagnostic
\a.[{\bf Nora: }] \emph{Is Greg, who bought a new car, envied by his neighbor?}\z.
Question to participants: Is Nora asking whether Greg bought a new car?
\z.

\begin{samepage}
  \ex. \label{dd} Direct-dissent diagnostic%
  \a.[{\bf Nora: }] \emph{Greg, who bought a new car, is envied by his neighbor.}
  \b.[{\bf Leo: }] \emph{No, he didn't buy a new car.}
  \z.
  Question to participants: How natural is Leo's rejection of Nora's utterance?
  \z.
\end{samepage}

\ex. \label{yesbut}%
`yes, but' diagnostic %
\a.[{\bf Nora: }] \emph{Greg, who bought a new car, is envied by his neighbor.}
\b.[{\bf Leo: }] \emph{Yes, but he didn't buy a new car.} /
\b.[] \hspace*{0em} \emph{Yes, and he didn't buy a new car.} /
\b.[] \hspace*{0em} \emph{No, he didn't buy a new car.}
\z.
Task for participants: Choose the response that sounds best.
\z.

The QUD-diagnostic in \ref{qud} targets property \ref{properties.i}  by testing whether the target content can felicitously address an established QUD. It presents the content in a response to a question that makes the target content relevant and elicits ratings of question-answer fit (for applications, see, e.g., \citealt{amaral_review_2007,lee_evidentiality_2011,tonhauser_diagnosing_2012,chen_presuppositions_2024}). Responses are expected to indicate a good fit when the content can be construed as at-issue and a bad fit otherwise. Accordingly, responses are expected to indicate a bad fit for the  NRRC in \ref{qud}.

The `asking whether' diagnostic in \ref{aw}  targets property \ref{properties.ii} by probing whether the target content determines the alternatives of a polar interrogative (for applications, see, e.g., \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018,solstad_cataphoric_2024,degen_projection_2025}). It presents the target content in a polar interrogative, and elicits judgments about whether the speaker's question is about that content. These judgments are expected to indicate that the speaker is asking about the target content  for at-issue content and that the speaker is not asking about the target content otherwise. Accordingly, participants are expected to judge that the speaker is not asking about the NRRC content in \ref{aw}.

The direct-dissent diagnostic in \ref{dd}  and the `yes, but' diagnostic in \ref{yesbut} target property \ref{properties.iii} (for applications, see, e.g., \citealt{faller_semantics_2002,faller_evidentiality_2006,papafragou_epistemic_2006,amaral_review_2007,murray_evidentiality_2010,murray_varieties_2014,anderbois_crossing_2010,anderbois_at-issue_2015,tonhauser_diagnosing_2012,syrett_experimental_2015} and \citealt{xue_correlation_2011,cummins_backgrounding_2013,destruel_cross-linguistic_2015}). Both present the target content in a declarative sentence and assume that only at-issue content can be directly assented or dissented with. %, whereas dissenting with not-at-issue content requires more indirect discourse moves.
%
The direct-dissent diagnostic elicits naturalness ratings for a response directly dissenting with the target content; the response is expected to indicate naturalness for at-issue content and unnaturalness for not-at-issue content. Accordingly,  responses indicating unnaturalness are expected for the NRRC content in \ref{dd}.
%
The `yes, but' diagnostic presents a forced choice between \emph{`no'}, \emph{`yes, but'}, and \emph{`yes, and'} when denying the target content. Under the assumption that both \emph{`yes'}-responses provide indirect denials suitable for dissenting with not-at-issue content (\citealt{xue_correlation_2011}), participants are expected to prefer one of the \emph{`yes'}-responses when denying not-at-issue content, such as the NRRC content in \ref{yesbut}. On the other hand, participants are expected to prefer the direct denial with \emph{`no'} when dissenting with at-issue content.%
%
\footnote{%
Although at-issue content permits direct dissent, it does not require it, and indirect dissent is also possible (see, e.g., \citealt{cummins_backgrounding_2013} on \citepos{shanon_two_1976} \enquote{Hey, wait a minute} diagnostic).
Work in conversation analysis suggests that preferences for direct versus indirect dissent vary with context and content. Direct dissent is often overall dispreferred (\citealt{sacks_preferences_1987,brown_politeness_1978,pomerantz_agreeing_1984,locher_power_2004}), but the strength of this preference can vary with cultural norms (e.g., \citealt{kakava_opposition_2002}) or speaker gender (e.g., \citealt{tannen_you_1990}). In contrast, direct dissent can be preferred in adversarial legal contexts (\citealt{atkinson_order_1979}), in disputes (\citealt{kotthoff_disagreement_1993,kangasharju_preference_2009}), or in responses to self-deprecating statements (\citealt{pomerantz_agreeing_1984}).
}

%\subsection{Diagnostic differences and notions of at-issueness}

At present, there is no consensus on whether the two definitions of at-issueness in \ref{def:qai} and \ref{def:proposal} define the same concept, or whether diagnostics based on the three properties in \ref{properties} probe the same phenomenon. Some works suggest a unified view. For instance, \citet{anderbois_at-issue_2015} and \citet{faller_discourse_2019} suggested that if proposing content for the common ground is intrinsically linked to managing the QUD (e.g., \citealt{farkas_reacting_2010}), the proposal-based definition in \ref{def:proposal} can be seen as a special case of the QUD-based definition in \ref{def:qai} when applied to assertions. And, as mentioned above already, \citet{tonhauser_diagnosing_2012} suggested that diagnostics based on the three properties in \ref{properties} can be taken to probe a QUD-based definition of at-issueness.

On the other hand, there are works that have argued that the definitions in \ref{def:qai} and \ref{def:proposal} characterize different conceptions of at-issueness that are probed by different diagnostics, or that only some of the diagnostics probe at-issueness, whereas others are sensitive to anaphoric availability (e.g., \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017,koev_notions_2018,korotkova_evidential_2020}). These positions are supported by the observation that different diagnostics may give different results for particular types of content. One such case involves the content of sentence-medial and -final NRRCs. In a forced-choice continuation experiment, \citet{syrett_experimental_2015} found that the content of sentence-final  NRRCs is more at-issue than that of medial ones, under a version of the direct-dissent diagnostic, which targets property \ref{properties.iii}. As illustrated in \Next, participants listened to (and read) utterances of declarative sentences introducing the target content (A) and chose between two \emph{no}-responses expressing direct dissent (which were only presented in writing), targeting the content of the main clause (B1) or of the NRRC (B2). \citet[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} reported that participants chose to dissent with the content of sentence-medial NRRCs in only 21.1\% of the trials whereas they chose to dissent with the content of sentence-final NRRCs in 35.5\%. These results suggest that the content of the sentence-final NRRCs in their study was more at-issue than that of medial ones.

\ex.\label{ex:sk} \citealt{syrett_experimental_2015}, p.~541
\a. Sentence-medial NRRC
\a.[A:] \emph{My friend Sophie, who performed a piece by Mozart, is a classical violinist.}
\b.[B1:] \emph{No, she's not.} \hfill (target: main clause)
\b.[B2:] \emph{No, she didn't.} \hfill (target: NRRC)
\z.
\b. Sentence-final NRRC
\a.[A:] \emph{The symphony hired my friend Sophie, who performed a piece by Mozart.}
\b.[B1:] \emph{No, they didn't.} \hfill (target: main clause)
\b.[B2:] \emph{No, she didn't.} \hfill (target: NRRC)
\z.\z.


\citet{snider_anaphoric_2017} has argued that this positional effect does not emerge with diagnostics based on properties \ref{properties.i} or \ref{properties.ii}. For instance, as illustrated in \ref{snider} for the QUD diagnostic, which is based on property \ref{properties.i}, neither the content of the sentence-medial NRRC in B1 nor the content of the sentence-final NRRC in B2 can address A's question. This observation suggests that both contents are not-at-issue according to the QUD diagnostic, contrasting with the results of \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015}. Snider has proposed that only the diagnostics based on properties \ref{properties.i} and \ref{properties.ii} genuinely probe at-issueness, whereas diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.iii} are instead sensitive to the availability of the target content for propositional anaphora, e.g., those contributed by response particles (see also \citealt{snider_at-issueness_2017,snider_anaphoric_2017,snider_distinguishing_2018,korotkova_evidential_2020}).

\ex.\label{snider} \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017}, p.~13
\a.[A:] \emph{Who is Margaret's cousin?}
\b.[B1:] \#\emph{Pauline, who is Margaret's cousin, was interviewed by Food Network.}
\b.[B2:] \#\emph{Food Network interviewed Pauline, who is Margaret's cousin.} 
\z.\z.

%Furthermore, \citet{snider_anaphoric_2017} suggested that the observation that diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.iii} do not yield the same results as diagnostics based on properties \ref{properties.i} and \ref{properties.ii} means that diagnostics targeting properties \ref{properties.i} and \ref{properties.ii} genuinely probe at-issueness, whereas diagnostics targeting property \ref{properties.iii} are instead sensitive to the availability of the target content for propositional anaphora (see also \citealt{snider_at-issueness_2017,snider_distinguishing_2018,korotkova_evidential_2020}). 

Similarly, \citet{koev_notions_2018} has pointed out that diagnostics corresponding to QUD-based and proposal-based definitions of at-issueness may yield different results. For instance, while the content of a slifting parenthetical like \emph{her fianc\'e claimed} can be directly dissented with, as illustrated in \ref{koeva}, the content of the slifting parenthetical \emph{she announced} in \ref{koevb} cannot address the QUD. These data again suggest that diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.i} do not always give the same results as diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.iii}. \citealt{koev_notions_2018} suggests that the QUD-based definition of at-issueness and the proposal-based one ``appear to be somewhat independent notions''  (p.12).

\ex.\label{koev} \citealt{koev_notions_2018}, p.~11
\a.\label{koeva} 
\a.[A:] \emph{Ellen is a passionate cook, her fianc\'e claimed.}
\b.[B:] \emph{No, he didn't.}
\z.
\b.\label{koevb}
\a.[Q:] \emph{What did she do next?}
\b.[A:] \#\emph{Her husband was a real sweetheart, she announced.}
\c.[A$'$:] \emph{She announced that her husband was a real sweetheart.}
\z. \z.


Finally, a mixed picture emerged in \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018}. This work compared the `asking whether' diagnostic in \ref{aw}, based on property \ref{properties.ii}, with a diagnostic referred to as the `are you sure?' diagnostic,  based on property \ref{properties.iii}. On this latter diagnostic, participants read dialogues, like the one in \Next with a sentence-medial NRRC, and provide ratings about whether the final response (by Fred) addresses the {\em Are you sure?}-question (by Carla); this diagnostic is similar to the `really' diagnostic of \citealt{shanon_two_1976}. The diagnostic was taken in \citealt[519]{tonhauser_how_2018} to be based on property \ref{properties.iii} because it tests whether the target content (in \Next, that Mike visited Alcatraz) can be directly challenged by the \emph{Are you sure?}-question. For the NRRC  in \Next, the diagnostic is expected to yield responses that indicate that the response is not addressing the question.

\ex. \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018}, p. 535\\
Fred: \emph{Mike, who visited Alcatraz, is a history fan.}\\
Carla: \emph{Are you sure?}\\
Fred: \emph{Yes, I am sure that Mike visited Alcatraz.}\\
Question to participants: Did Fred answer Carla's question?
\z.

%Beyond NRRCs, \citet{tonhauser_how_2018} compared the `asking whether' and `are you sure?' diagnostics for contents associated with several English expressions, including sentence-medial NRRCs and complements of the clause-embedding predicates \emph{annoyed, discover} and \emph{know} \Next.

%\ex. \citet{tonhauser_how_2018}, p. 503
%\a. annoyed: \hfill \emph{Martha's neighbor is annoyed that Martha has a new BMW.}
%\b. discover:\hfill \emph{Mary discovered that her daughter has been biting her nails.}
%\b. know: \hfill \emph{Billy knows that Martha has a new BMW.}
%\z. \z. 

On the basis of two experiments that investigated the at-issueness of contents associated with 19 English expressions, including sentence-medial NRRCs and the complements of clause-embedding predicates, including \emph{annoyed, discover} and \emph{know}, \citet{tonhauser_how_2018} found both convergence and divergence in their comparison of the results of the two diagnostics (p.~526ff.). On the one hand, they observed that mean ratings were correlated quite strongly across the two diagnostics ($r$=.62), suggesting that ``the two ways of operationalizing at-issueness measure the same underlying concept'' (p.~526). On the other hand, the `asking whether' diagnostic yielded lower overall ratings and smaller differentiation between contents than the `are you sure?' diagnostic. Furthermore, for particular pairs of contents, the two diagnostics  gave different results. For instance, the pre-state of \emph{stop} was the most at-issue content on the `asking whether' diagnostic in their Exp.~1a, but not on the `are you sure?' diagnostic in Exp.~2a. Overall, the results of \citepos{tonhauser_how_2018} comparison suggest that diagnostics based on properties \ref{properties.ii} and \ref{properties.iii} might probe the same underlying concept of at-issueness even though the diagnostics do not yield fully identical results.

In sum, it is an open question whether different diagnostics of at-issueness yield consistent results and, consequently, also whether different diagnostics probe the same underlying concept. This paper contributes to addressing these two research questions, which are summarized in \ref{rqs}:

\ex.\label{rqs} Research questions
\a.\label{rqa} Do different diagnostics of at-issueness yield consistent results?
\b.\label{rqb} Are different diagnostics of at-issueness sensitive to the same underlying concept?
\z.

To address \ref{rqa}, we compare diagnostics by testing whether they reproduce by-content contrasts reported in prior work, namely the medial-final contrast for NRRCs observed in \citealt{syrett_experimental_2015}, and differences among the embedded contents of clause-embedding predicates such as the ones included in \citet{tonhauser_how_2018} and subsequent work. In particular, we test whether the diagnostics reproduce fine-grained differences among predicates reported in \citet{degen_projection_2025}, introduced in the next section.

To begin addressing \ref{rqb}, we consider how differences across diagnostics should be interpreted. Such differences would provide evidence that the diagnostics track distinct properties of at-issue content, only if they are not explained by other factors, like the meaning of the expressions conveying the content, the presentation of the stimuli, item choice, or the response task.

Following prior work (e.g., \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018}), we describe higher/lower mean ratings by saying that content is more/less at-issue under a given diagnostic. However, we remain agnostic about whether at-issueness is an underlyingly binary or a gradient property (for discussion, see, e.g., \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018,barnes_information_2023}). On a gradient view, gradient mean ratings may be taken to reflect gradient at-issueness. On a categorical view, gradient mean ratings could be taken to reflect uncertainty about what the relevant QUD or proposal is, thus indicating the frequency or ease with which a particular QUD or assertive speech act is attributed to the utterance.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion1}

Exps.~1--4 were designed to compare the four at-issueness diagnostics in \ref{qud} to \ref{yesbut}, which have been widely used in the literature. The results of Exps.~1--4 suggest that diagnostics for at-issueness are not readily interchangeable. This section discusses these results. %, and in the relative order of the seven contents investigated. Three main differences between the four experiments emerged:

\subsubsection{At-issueness differences between sentence-medial vs.~-final NRRCs} \label{ssub:s}

We begin with the question of why the results of none of our Exps.~1--4 replicated the difference between sentence-medial and -final NRRCs observed in \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015}. %NRRCs was relatively more at-issue than that of sentence-medial ones, on the basis of an experiment that implemented a direct-dissent diagnostic in which participants had to choose between a direct dissent with the content of the NRRC or of the main clause, as illustrated in \ref{ex:sk}. None of our Exps.~1--4 replicated this finding: Exps.~1--3 found no positional difference, while Exp.~4 (`yes, but') showed the opposite pattern. 

For Exp.~2 (asking whether'), we hypothesize that the absence of a positional effect may be due to an interaction between the diagnostic and the illocutionary properties of NRRCs, namely that they contribute a speech act that is separate from the speech act conveyed by their host sentence (see, e.g., \citealt{potts_logic_2005,koev_apposition_2013,syrett_experimental_2015}). Under this view, the sample stimuli of Exp.~2 shown  in \ref{ex:nrrc-AW} convey two distinct speech acts: the content of the NRRC is an assertion and the main clause content is an (information-seeking) question.

\ex.\label{ex:nrrc-AW} Sentence-medial NRRC: \hfill \emph{Does Greg, who bought a new car, have a driver's license?}
\\ Sentence-final NRRC: \hfill  \emph{Does the neighbor envy Greg, who bought a new car?}\smallskip
\\ Question for participants: Is the speaker asking whether Greg bought a car?
\z.

If the  NRRC contributes a separate assertion rather than being part of the question, participants would not be expected to judge the speaker as asking about its content, regardless of whether it is contributed sentence-medially or -finally. This hypothesis is consistent with the very low mean ratings for both medial and sentence-final NRRCs in Exp.~2. %In contrast, the declarative stimuli in Exps.~1, 3, and 4 present both the main-clause content and the  as assertions, making them more similar to the declarative stimuli used by \citet{syrett_experimental_2015}.

In the other three experiments, target contents were presented in declarative main clauses, which contribute assertions. In discussing their findings, \citet{syrett_experimental_2015} proposed that NRRC content competes with that of the main clause for at-issue status. %If this analysis is correct, and if such competition requires the competing contents to have the same illocutionary force, then the absence of a positional effect in Exp.~2 is expected: an  that contributes an assertion may not be in a position to compete for at-issueness status with an interrogative main clause. Since Syrett and Koev attribute the positional effect to the influence of linear order on this competition, this kind of explanation could provide an understanding of why the positional effect is absent at least in Exp.~2, where the target content was presented in an interrogative main clause. 
%
They attribute the positional effect to the influence of linear order on this competition: sentence-final NRRCs in declarative sentences can be asserted after the main-clause assertion is completed, in which case they are assumed to have a discourse-effect \enquote{similar that of having two independent sentences that follow upon each other} (p.298).
%
If so, NRRC content should, in principle, be able to compete with the content of the main clause for at-issue status in Exps.~1, 3 and 4, where target contents were presented in declarative sentences. However, as pointed out above, the results of these experiments do not support the positional difference observed in \citealt{syrett_experimental_2015} either. This is true even for the dissent-based diagnostics implemented in Exps.~3 (direct dissent) and Exp.~4 (`yes, but'), which most closely resemble the diagnostic used in Exp.~2 of \citet{syrett_experimental_2015}, as they all rely on property \ref{properties.iii} of at-issue content.

There are several differences between the experiments that may have contributed to this divergence in results. One of them are the the items used. Another one is that the target sentences in \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} were not only presented as visual written stimuli, as in our Exps.~1--4, but also auditorily. Another difference concerns the response task. Although \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015}  and our Exps.~3 and 4 used dissent-based diagnostics with target content presented in declarative assertions, the response tasks of the three experiments differed. Specifically, \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} forced participants to choose between a dissent with the content of the NRRC and that of the main clause, Exp.~3 elicited naturalness ratings on question/answer pairs, and Exp.~4 used a force choice task, but the response options differed  from those in \citealt{syrett_experimental_2015} in that participants had to choose between different forms of dissent with the content of the NRRC. %ejected the target content, and participants chose between expressing direct dissent (\emph{no}), or indirect dissent (with \emph{yes, but} or \emph{yes, and}). In contrast, in Syrett \& Koev's study, all response options expressed direct dissent (with \emph{no}), and participants chose whether to reject the NRRC content or the main clause content.
%Taken together, these results suggest that positional effects for NRRCs may not be robust across diagnostics and, furthermore, the corresponding task differences suggest that even small changes in the response task used to operationalize a diagnostic, such forced-choice alternatives, could reverse the observed pattern. More generally, different diagnostics vary in whether they detect any positional difference for NRRCs at all. This suggests that previously reported effects may reflect task- or diagnostic-specific effects rather than stable properties of NRRC interpretation. There are a number of differences between the three diagnostics that may have contributed to these different results: the stimuli, the response task, the response options. It is a pressing question for future research to identify whether the results obtained in the three experiments replicate and, if yes, why these three diagnostics give distinct results. 
A natural follow-up would be to investigate whether the diagnostic as implemented in \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} applied to our stimuli replicates the distinction between sentence-medial and -final NRRCs that they observed, and whether presenting spoken vs.~written stimuli is critical. Importantly, we argue in \S\ref{sec:4_discussion} that it would be premature to conclude from these differences in the results that these diagnostics are not sensitive to the same underlying concept.


\subsubsection{An interaction between the QUD diagnostic and the meaning of \emph{be right} \label{ssub:be-right}}

\section{General discussion \label{sec:4_discussion}}

This paper reported the results of six experiments to address two research questions, namely \ref{rqa} whether different diagnostics of at-issueness yield consistent results, and \ref{rqb} whether different diagnostics of at-issueness are sensitive to the same underlying concept. In this section, we discuss in \S\ref{ssec:rqa} what the results of the six experiments suggest regarding the research question in \ref{rqa}, and in \S\ref{ssec:rqb} what they suggest regarding the research question in \ref{rqb}. Finally, in \S\ref{ssec:discussion-methods}, we present methodological implications of the results. 

\subsection{Do different diagnostics of at-issueness yield consistent results?}\label{ssec:rqa}

We begin to address research question \ref{rqa} by taking stock. On the one hand, there are several results that suggest a negative answer to the research question: For the contents of sentence-medial and -final NRRCs, the results of Exps.~1--4 did not replicate \citepos{syrett_experimental_2015} result, that sentence-final NRRCs are more at-issue than -medial ones. This result resonates with \citepos{snider_anaphoric_2017} observation, discussed in \S\ref{sec:1_introduction}, that not all at-issueness diagnostics show a positional effect for NRRCs. Likewise, \citealt{koev_notions_2018} pointed out that the direct dissent and the QUD diagnostic give different results for the content of slifting parentheticals, as also discussed in \S\ref{sec:1_introduction}. Finally, for the contents of the complements of the five clause-embedding predicates, only the results Exp.~2 (`asking whether') came close to replicating the results of \citealt{degen_projection_2025}, which also used the `asking whether' diagnostic. These results suggest that different diagnostics do not necessarily yield consistent results.

On the other hand, the results of Exps.~5--6 may be taken to suggest a positive answer to research question \ref{rqa}: The results of these two experiments were very similar even though they differed in the diagnostic implemented and the response task; crucially, while the diagnostic implemented in Exp.~5 only tapped into property \ref{properties.ii}, the diagnostic implemented in Exp.~6 also tapped into property \ref{properties.iii}. The results of \citealt{tonhauser_how_2018} also suggested that the `asking whether' and the `are you sure?' diagnostics (which also rely on distinct properties of at-issue content, namely property \ref{properties.ii} and \ref{properties.iii}, respectively) may ``measure the same underlying concept'' (p.~526), even though there also were some differences in the results of the two diagnostics. These results suggest that different diagnostics can yield consistent results. 

Taken together, the results of Exps.~1--6 and those reported in prior literature suggest that the answer to research question \ref{rqa} is (an unsatisfying) ``maybe'': Different diagnostics may yield different results when applied to the same contents, but they also may yield consistent results. An important next step in research on at-issueness is to identify when diagnostics give different results and when they do not. The research reported on in this paper already provides some useful pointers in this regard. First, even when two diagnostics rely on the same property of at-issue content, different response tasks may lead to different results (see, e.g., \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} vs.~Exps.~3--4). Second, realizing target contents in polar interrogatives (as in Exps.~2, 5--6) may lead to greater differentiation between some contents (like the contents of the complements of clause-embedding predicates) than realizing target contents in positive declaratives (as in Exps.~1, 3--4). Third, when target contents are realized in polar interrogatives, it may not matter whether the response task taps into property \ref{properties.ii} (as in Exps.~2 and 5) or into property \ref{properties.iii} (as in Exp.~6).

Particularly important is investigating when two diagnostics that rely on distinct properties of at-issue content yield the same or different results. If we were able to unambiguously conclude that two diagnostics yield different results because they are based on different properties of at-issue content, this result would provide empirical support for the assumption that the diagnostics do not probe the same underlying concept of at-issueness. The next section follows up on this point.

\subsection{Do different diagnostics probe the same underlying concept?}\label{ssec:rqb}

This section addresses research question \ref{rqb}, that is, whether different diagnostics of at-issueness probe the same underlying concept. We begin again by taking stock. For NRRCs, the diagnostics implemented in Exps.~1--4 did not provide empirical support for the result of \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015}, that the content of sentence-final NRRCs is more at-issue than that of -medial ones; see also \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017}. Should we conclude from this result that the diagnostic implemented in \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} measures a different underlying concept than the diagnostics implemented in Exps.~1--4?
% , like \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017} did?

We argue that this conclusion would be premature, as the differences in the results may also be due to different interactions between the meanings of the expressions associated with the target contents and the diagnostics. For instance, we suggested in \S\ref{ssub:s} that the `asking whether' diagnostic implemented in Exp.~2 may not be suitable for detecting differences between sentence-medial and -final NRRCs, due to the fact that NRRCs contribute their own speech act which differs from the question contributed by the interrogative. We also pointed out several differences between \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} and our Exps.~3--4 (which all relied on property \ref{properties.iii}), which may have contributed to the differences in the results. Therefore, as suggested in the previous section, in order to claim that two diagnostics do not measure the same underlying concept, we need to establish that differences in the results are unambiguously due to the diagnostics relying on distinct properties of at-issue content and cannot be attributed to other factors, like the meaning of the expression associated with the content, the presentation of the stimuli, the items or the response task.

For the contents of the complements of clause-embedding predicates, the diagnostics implemented in Exps.~1--4 also did not give consistent results: Recall that the results of Exp.~2 (`asking whether') provided empirical support for 9 out of 10 possible pairwise differences that were observed in \citealt{degen_projection_2025}, Exp.~5 (`asking whether') and Exp.~6 (affirmative response). In contrast, the results of Exps.~1 (QUD)  and 4 (`yes, but') only supported 2 and 5 of the 10 possible pairwise differences, and the results of Exp.~3 (direct-dissent) supported none. We again argue that concluding from these results that the diagnostics implemented in Exps.~1, 3, and 4 measure a different underlying concept than those implemented in Exps.~2, 5, and 6 would be premature. Rather, as we hypothesized in \S\ref{ssub:differentiation}, it is possible that polar interrogatives interact differently with the meanings of the expressions and the target contents than positive declaratives. In other words, the diagnostics implemented in Exps.~1--6 may measure the same underlying concept but differences emerge for the contents of the complements of clause-embedding predicates because of different interactions with polar interrogatives and positive declaratives.

In sum, with respect to research question \ref{rqb}, we conclude that we are not yet in a position to determine whether different diagnostics measure the same underlying concept. If one were to observe that two diagnostics yield consistent results for any type of content they are applied to, that may be taken to provide support for the position that the two diagnostics measure the same underlying concept. But when two diagnostics don't (always) yield consistent results when applied to a broad range of contents, what is needed to determine whether the diagnostics measure the same underlying concept, or two different ones, is a deeper understanding of the workings of the diagnostic and its interaction with the meanings of expressions associated with the contents. These are important tasks for future research on at-issueness.

\subsection{Methodological implications \label{ssec:discussion-methods}}

There are several methodological implications that emerge from the results of the six experiments. A first implication is that the results of research on at-issueness may be particular to the diagnostic used in the research and may not generalize to other diagnostics. This methodological implication was already suggested in \citealt{snider_at-issueness_2017,snider_anaphoric_2017,snider_distinguishing_2018}, \citealt{koev_notions_2018} and \citealt{korotkova_evidential_2020}. In our work, the methodological implication follows straightforwardly from the observation that the results of Exps.~1--4 differed for the contents of the complements of the five clause-embedding predicates. It also follows from the observation that none of Exps.~1--4 replicated the differences between sentence-medial and -final NRRCs reported in \citealt{syrett_experimental_2015}, including even Exps.3--4, which also implemented diagnostics based on property \ref{properties.iii}.

A second methodological implication is that it is important to consider the response task with  which a diagnostic is implemented. There are several pairs of experiments that suggest this methodological implication. For instance, while both our Exp.~4 and \citealt[Exp.~2]{syrett_experimental_2015} implemented a dissent diagnostic, they different in the response task and yielded slightly different results. Likewise, Exps.~5 and 6 both embedded the target contents in polar interrogatives, but differed in the response task and yielded slightly different results for some pairs of clause-embedding predicates. Thus, when designing an experiment to measure at-issueness, it is important to consider not just the diagnostic, but also the response task.

A third methodological implication is that the expressions and items used in an experiment may yield different result. This implication follows from our comparison of the results of Exp.~2 (`asking whether') and Exps.~5--6. Whereas the latter two experiments provided support for all ten pairwise differences between the five clause-embedding predicates investigated in Exps.~1--4, the results of Exp.~2 only provided support for nine of the ten differences. One salient difference between Exp.~2 and Exp.~5 are the expressions investigated in the two experiments and them items. As noted in \S\ref{sec:3_more-experiments}, Exp.~5 also consisted of a projection block, which may also have affected the results (see \citealt{degen_projection_2025}).

A final methodological implication is that some diagnostics may not be suitable for some types of contents. Recall from \S\ref{ssub:be-right} that we hypothesized that the content of the complement of \emph{be right} was rated as rather unacceptable in Exp.~1 (QUD diagnostic) not because it is not-at-issue but because of the way in which the meaning of \emph{be right} interacts with the diagnostic. Another result that could suggest this methodological implication comes from the observation that Exp.~2 (`asking whether') did not show differentiation between the contents of sentence-medial and -final NRRCs. We suggested in \S\ref{ssub:s} that this is due to NRRCs contributing their own speech act, which differs from the speech act of the main clause in polar interrogatives. In sum, both of these results suggest that some diagnostics may not be suitable for some types of contents.

\section{Conclusions \label{sec:5_conclusion}}

At-issueness is a key concept in theoretical semantics and pragmatics, but there is no consensus to date on whether different diagnostics measure the same or distinct underlying concepts, and how at-issueness is defined. This paper contributed to these overarching questions by experimentally comparing five diagnostics of at-issueness applied to the contents of NRRCs and of the complements of clause-embedding predicates. The results gave a mixed picture, as they suggest that some of the diagnostics are not interchangeable while others are. We cautioned against taking the former result to mean that the diagnostics measure distinct underlying concepts (as done in, e.g., \citealt{snider_anaphoric_2017} and \citealt{koev_notions_2018}) because differences in the results of different diagnostics may also be the result of an interaction between the diagnostics and the meanings of the expressions whose contents are diagnosed. Ultimately, we proposed that further investigations are needed to determine whether different diagnostics measure the same underlying concept and the paper offered several methodological implications for future research on at-issueness.
